What changes drainage scope on an acre lot below the foothills

Big lots and steady slopes mean each parcel needs its own plan. These details move the scope most.

An arroyo or flood zone on the parcel
Either can limit where you build and grade, and may bring in rules from the city, county or flood control authority.
Uphill water
Runoff from the slope above often crosses your lot, so swales or berms may be needed to steer it around the house site.
Rock and caliche
Cutting swales or ponding areas through hard ground can take a ripper or hydraulic hammer.
Built or vacant
A raw lot lets us set drainage along with the pad; an existing home means working around what's already there.
Native vegetation you want to keep
Clearing only what's needed protects the ground cover that already holds the soil.

We stay out of the arroyo

Arroyos crossing North Albuquerque Acres lots can carry fast, dangerous water during storms. Work in or near one usually needs approval, and we don't work inside an arroyo without the required permissions. If part of your lot may be in a mapped flood zone, confirm that before you plan the site.

Building drainage into a new custom-home site

On a vacant lot, drainage is easiest to get right as part of site prep.

  1. 1

    Start from the plans

    We work from your engineer's grading and drainage plan and the builder's staking. Larger or more complex sites often need one.

  2. 2

    Locate and clear

    We request a New Mexico 811 locate and clear only the native brush the build and drainage require.

  3. 3

    Set the pad and the fall

    The pad is built and compacted in lifts at the planned elevation, and the ground around it is shaped to fall away on every side.

  4. 4

    Build the water's route

    Swales, berms and rock rundowns carry runoff from uphill around the house and toward the planned outlet or retention area.

  5. 5

    Stabilize

    Rock, mulch or wattles cover disturbed ground so the first monsoon doesn't undo the work.

Fixing drainage on homes that are already built

Some established homes here were graded well and have settled since. Others never had a clear path for the water coming off the slope above. The signs look alike: gullies along the driveway, decomposed granite washing against a back wall, or water reaching the garage door.

On a finished lot, we usually add a swale or berm upslope of the house, armor the spots where water concentrates with rock, and fine-tune the grade near the foundation. A culvert under a long driveway can also stop runoff from cutting across it.

Questions to settle before grading starts

  • Is your parcel under city or county rules? It varies within the area.
  • Does your subdivision have an HOA or architectural guidelines covering grading or landscaping?
  • Is any part of the lot in a mapped flood zone?
  • Does the build call for an engineered grading and drainage plan?
  • Will the job disturb enough ground that a fugitive dust control permit may apply?

Drainage & erosion questions in Albuquerque Acres

Can I put a retention pond on my lot instead of sending water to the street?

Often, yes. Large lots here have room for a ponding area that catches runoff and lets it soak in. Its size and location should fit your grading plan and any rules on your parcel.

Will drainage work block my Sandia views?

It shouldn't. Swales, berms and rock rundowns sit low to the ground, and we keep berms modest and shaped to blend with the native landscape.

Does the North Albuquerque Acres Community Association approve drainage work?

We can't speak for the association. Rules here vary by parcel and subdivision, and some subdivisions have their own HOA or architectural guidelines, so check with whichever group covers your lot.
