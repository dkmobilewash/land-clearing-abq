What a Rio Rancho dig usually looks like

Footings on a freshly cleared lot

The brush just came off and the builder is ready. Mesa sand digs quickly, so the trenches are cut shortly before forms go in rather than left open for days.

A build in Rio Rancho Estates

The lot sits on an unpaved road with no turnaround. We may cut a temporary access or turnaround first so excavator trailers and dump trucks can get in and out.

Addition in an established neighborhood

Closer-in streets mean block walls, side yards and neighbors on both sides. Access width decides the machine, and the haul plan keeps sand out of the street.

Caliche partway down

Some lots are sand for the first stretch and then turn hard. When the bucket stops, we switch to ripping or hammering instead of fighting it.

Mesa sand moves, so the dig is planned around it

Sandy soil is forgiving to dig and unforgiving to leave. Open trench walls slump, and spoil piles start blowing as soon as the afternoon wind picks up.

We keep piles low and close to where they'll be reused, wet them down when the job calls for it, and backfill in compacted lifts so the sand around a new foundation doesn't settle later.

What we watch on a sandy lot

  • Trench and footing walls that begin to slough
  • Wind on the forecast for digging days
  • Spoil piles near property lines and roads
  • Caliche lenses below the sand
  • Moisture during compaction of backfill

What changes the effort on a mesa-lot excavation

Two lots a few streets apart can quote very differently. These are the things that make the difference.

Road and lot access
Soft or unpaved roads, washouts and no place to turn a truck add time and sometimes a prep step.
Spoil volume and distance
Basements produce a lot of dirt. Whether it stays on the lot or gets hauled out matters.
Fill to raise a pad
If the plan sets the house higher than existing grade, fill has to be brought in and compacted.
Hard layers
Caliche shows up on some lots and not others, and it changes the equipment needed.
Water on site
Raw lots often have no water hookup yet, so water for compaction and dust may need to be trucked in.

City of Rio Rancho rules, and your corner pins

Rio Rancho sets its own building and development requirements, separate from Albuquerque, and excavation is usually one piece of a permitted project. Check with the city before you dig. On older lots the survey pins can be buried or missing, so have a surveyor mark them before footings are staked. We also request a New Mexico 811 locate ahead of every dig.

Excavation questions in Rio Rancho

My Rio Rancho lot is on a dirt road. Can your equipment reach it?

In most cases, yes. We check the road at the estimate. If it's soft or there's no room to turn, we'll suggest cutting access or a turnaround before the excavation starts.

Can you bring in fill to raise a building pad?

Yes. When the plans call for a higher pad, we import fill and compact it in lifts to the elevation your builder or engineer specifies.

Do my survey pins need to be found before footings are dug?

It's a good idea. Footings are staked from the property lines, and old pins out here are often buried or gone. A surveyor can locate or reset them first.
